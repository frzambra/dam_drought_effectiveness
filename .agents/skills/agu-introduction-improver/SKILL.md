# AGU introduction improver

Use this skill when a manuscript introduction needs to be tightened, clarified, or reframed for a hydrology, water resources, or Earth-system journal audience. The goal is not just better prose. The goal is to make the introduction do the core AGU job: establish why the problem matters, show what is known, show what is missing, justify the study design, and make the contribution feel necessary and defensible.

## Core objective

An AGU introduction should move the reader from a broad, important scientific context to a precise and defensible gap, then to the study's specific contribution. It should sound like a scientific argument, not a literature summary or a prose dump of every paper the team has read.

## Workflow

1. Identify the paper's central contribution
   - State the main claim in one sentence.
   - Identify the actual causal or mechanistic question being answered.
   - Separate the main result from the broader framing narrative.
   - Check whether the manuscript is claiming a process, a counterfactual, a method, or a policy-relevant result.

2. Map the introduction into its required rhetorical roles
   - Why does the problem matter to the scientific community?
   - What is already known?
   - What remains unresolved or contested?
   - Why is the chosen setting or system a strong test case?
   - What is the study's unique contribution and design?

3. Audit the literature synthesis
   - Confirm the introduction cites only the papers that support the argument being made.
   - Distinguish established findings from open questions.
   - Correct any overstatement such as "has never been tested" when similar work exists.
   - Make the gap specific: not a generic statement, but a precise missing comparison, counterfactual, mechanism, or scale.

4. Check the causal and identification logic
   - Ask whether the paper is making a causal claim or a descriptive claim.
   - If causal inference is central, make the identification strategy visible and defensible.
   - Ensure the introduction explains why the chosen design resolves the confounding problem prior literature has not addressed.
   - Avoid claims that imply certainty of mechanism when the design only narrows the plausible explanations.

5. Tighten the logic of the narrative arc
   - Open with a broad but relevant context.
   - Narrow to the specific process or phenomenon.
   - Explain the methodological obstacle or analytical ambiguity.
   - Present the study's design as the response to that gap.
   - End with a clear preview of the paper's objective and contribution.

6. Evaluate the study-area motivation
   - Explain why this region, basin, or system is a strong testbed.
   - Link the regional setting to the scientific question rather than treating it as a local anecdote.
   - If the region is selected because it enables a powerful identifications strategy, state that explicitly.

7. Improve the prose without losing precision
   - Replace vague phrases with direct scientific language.
   - Remove redundant background and narrative drift.
   - Prefer short, declarative sentences that keep the argument moving.
   - Keep the introduction concise but not thin; AGU readers expect density and argument, not filler.

8. Run a final AGU-style review
   - Is the problem important?
   - Is the gap concrete and defensible?
   - Is the novelty fair and specific?
   - Does the opening frame the paper as a meaningful advance rather than a routine case study?
   - Does the conclusion of the introduction set up the methods and results cleanly?

## Decision points

- If the introduction reads mainly as a literature review, re-center it on the specific unresolved question and why it matters.
- If the paper uses a counterfactual or causal design, explain that design before discussing the study system in detail.
- If the literature is mixed or contested, present the disagreement explicitly and explain how the manuscript resolves it.
- If the study region is unusual, justify it as a strong test of the mechanism rather than as a place with interesting local conditions only.
- If the paper claims novelty, make sure the novelty is in the causal question, identification strategy, setting, or synthesis, not simply in the fact that the problem is studied in a new place.
- If the opening is too broad, narrow it to the exact hydrological or Earth-system process at stake.
- If the closing paragraph is simply a summary of methods, replace it with a statement of the exact contribution and question the paper will answer.

## Quality criteria

A strong AGU introduction should have all of the following:

- A clear scientific problem with real importance to the field.
- A precise and defensible gap in the literature.
- A believable explanation of why the study system is informative.
- A clear distinction between what is known, what is contested, and what the paper will resolve.
- Causal or mechanistic language that matches the actual evidence and design.
- No unsupported overclaims, exaggerated novelty, or rhetorical inflation.
- A strong transition into the methods and results.

## Output expected

Return one of the following:

1. A revised introduction draft with improved narrative logic and sharper AGU framing.
2. A structured review memo identifying paragraph-level issues, the missing gap statement, and suggested rewrites.
3. A targeted rewrite of the opening, gap, and contribution paragraphs while preserving the author's scientific voice.

## Suggested workflow for this project

For reservoir and drought manuscripts, pay special attention to the following:

- Distinguish operational buffering from siting effects.
- Explain the difference between a modeled counterfactual and a real-world comparison.
- Make the paper's identification strategy explicit early.
- Frame the study as a causal test rather than a descriptive inventory of reservoir effects.
- Tell the reader why the study region is a particularly informative case for that test.
- Make the contribution feel stronger by showing how the analysis resolves a specific ambiguity in previous reservoir-drought literature.
