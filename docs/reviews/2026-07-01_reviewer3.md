## Reviewer Comments 

1. Uncontrolled groundwater substitution effects The study acknowledges that unmeasured groundwater pumping could sustain cropland and evapotranspiration, potentially masking operational buffering. Because dammed basins are significantly more arid, they may have systematically different groundwater reliance or depletion rates during the megadrought compared to the matched controls. Without accounting for this, the null findings for cropland area and ET cannot be confidently attributed to a lack of reservoir-induced demand, as groundwater may be acting as a hidden buffer. The authors should incorporate satellite-derived groundwater proxy data (such as GRACE anomalies) or regional well-level data to control for differential groundwater depletion between the treated and control basins. 2. Unresolved baseline aridity confounding The study relies heavily on a parametric outcome-model adjustment to account for a severe baseline aridity confound (median P/PET 0.26 vs 0.80) because exact matching collapses the effective sample size. Relying on a linear log-aridity adjustment to extrapolate across a genuine regime gap is highly risky, as hydrological responses to drought are frequently non-linear and subject to aridity thresholds. While the authors note that restricting the analysis to the aridity-overlap subset leaves the streamflow gap unchanged, the fact that the water-rights ATT loses significance in this subset suggests the parametric adjustment may be masking true structural differences. The authors should present a fully non-parametric matching specification on the overlap subset for all outcomes, even at the cost of statistical power, to prove the effects are not artifacts of functional form misspecification. 

3. Qverbroad claims on reservoir resilience The authors conclude that reservoirs do not create resilience and that expanding storage is a supplyblind response. However, the study design explicitly evaluates 12-month accumulations to capture multi-year vulnerability, deliberately excluding the sub-seasonal (1-3 month) buffering that irrigation reservoirs are primarily designed to provide. Concluding that reservoirs fail to create resilience broadly oversteps the evidence, which only supports a lack of multi-year buffering under structural aridification. The causal language and policy conclusions in the abstract and discussion must be strictly scoped to clarify that reservoirs may still successfully perform their primary function of intra-annual seasonal buffering, even if they fail to buffer multi-year megadroughts. 

4. Justification of equivalence margin The authors anchor the +25% equivalence margin to a change of less than 0.5 standardized units in the drought index. However, because the estimand is a slope (change in SSI per unit change in SPEJ), the absolute effect on the SSI depends entirely on the magnitude of the SPEI deficit. The manuscript does not clearly demonstrate that a 25% change in the slope strictly bounds the absolute SSI change to <0.5 across the actual empirical distribution of SPEI deficits experienced during the megadrought. The authors should provide the maximum observed SPEI deficit and explicitly show the calculation confirming that a 25% slope alteration cannot exceed the 0.5 SSI threshold under the most extreme observed drought conditions. 

5. Spatial structure in permutation test The randomization inference permutes treatment labels within climate and aridity strata, assuming exchangeability of sub-watersheds. However, hydrological and meteorological data often exhibit strong spatial autocorrelation. If the underlying data are spatially dependent, a simple stratified permutation test may not preserve the spatial structure of the null distribution, potentially leading to incorrect p-values. The authors should clarify whether spatial autocorrelation was assessed in the residuals and consider employing a spatially restricted permutation approach (e.g., spatial block bootstrapping or spatial permutations) to ensure the null distribution accurately reflects the spatial dependence of the basins. 

6. Exploratory masking of ET data The transition from whole-basin ET to orchard-only ET appears to be a data-driven, post-hoc decision made after observing a pre-trend failure in the primary outcome. While the mechanistic justification (barren-land aridity confounding) is plausible, modifying the outcome definition after observing the data mixes exploratory and confirmatory analyses. To ensure statistical rigor, the authors should explicitly state whether the orchard ET analysis was pre-specified. If it was exploratory, the language should be softened to reflect this, and the limitations of post-hoc outcome redefinition should be discussed, as it can inflate the likelihood of finding a desired result. 

7. Sensitivity to winsorization threshold The authors apply a 99.5th percentile winsorization to the consumptive flow data to handle severe volume errors in the registry. While addressing extreme outliers is necessary, the specific choice of the 99.5th percentile is arbitrary and could potentially mask true high-volume water rights accruals in dammed basins. To ensure the robustness of the null finding for water rights volume, the authors should report a sensitivity analysis demonstrating that the treatment effect remains statistically non-significant across a reasonable range of winsorization thresholds (e.g., 95th, 99th, and 99.9th percentiles). 

8. Unaddressed snowmelt and geomorphological confounders 

The within-basin placebo test relies on comparing unregulated upstream reaches to regulated downstream reaches, using elevation as a proxy to adjust for comparability. While the elevation adjustment is rigorous, it may not fully capture the non-linear hydrological differences between steep, snowdominated Andean headwaters and the flatter, alluvial valleys where downstream gauges and agricultural demands are typically located. The authors should explicitly discuss how differences in snowmelt buffering, groundwater-surface water interactions, and channel geomorphology between these distinct basin zones might residually confound the upstreamdownstream transmission slope comparison. 

9. Limitations of MOD16 in irrigated arid regions The use of the MOD16 product to estimate actual evapotranspiration in irrigated orchards within arid basins introduces potential uncertainties. MOD16 algorithms primarily rely on meteorological inputs and vegetation indices, which often underestimate ET in heavily irrigated arid regions because they do not explicitly account for anthropogenic water applications that decouple soil moisture from local precipitation. The authors should discuss the limitations of MOD16 in this specific context and how potential underestimation of irrigation-driven ET might influence the forcingconditioned transmission slope estimates. 10. Omission of the 2022 Water Code reform The manuscript contextualizes the supply-side policy reflex and demand-side vulnerabilities within the framework of the 1981 Water Code. However, it omits the significant 2022 reform to the Water Code (Law 21.435), which introduced mechanisms for the state to prioritize human consumption, limit the duration of new rights, and exercise greater regulatory control during severe scarcity. Given that the study period extends to 2024 and the discussion makes explicit policy recommendations regarding state-managed allocation, the implications of this recent legislative shift must be integrated into the discussion of governance and future adaptation strategies. 

10. Elevation effect sign contradiction The text states that upstream gauges sit higher (a 0.54 km upstream-downstream elevation gap) and that the control elevation-transmission sensitivity is negative (-0.21 per km). This implies that higher elevations should have more negative transmission slopes, so the upstream slope should be more negative than the downstream slope by about 0.11 (0.54 * -0.21 = -0.11). The observed slopes are -0.20 upstream and -0.16 downstream, meaning the observed upstream-minusdownstream difference is -0.04. Both the expected elevation-induced difference (-0.11) and the observed difference (-0.04) are negative (they have the same sign). However, the text claims the expected difference is ‘opposite in sign’ to the observed difference. 

12. Missing volume outcome in Table 1 and Figure 3 The text asserts that both the water-rights count and the winsorized volume are shown with analytic intervals excluding zero in Figure 3 and Table 1. However, Figure 3 and Table 1 only report the count outcome (‘rights / 100 km/2 added’); the volume outcome does not appear in either.
