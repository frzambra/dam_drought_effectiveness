## Reviewer Comments 

1. Unmeasured groundwater substitution The conclusion that reservoirs do not buffer evapotranspiration or sustain cropland during drought is confounded by potential groundwater substitution. If farmers in dammed basins systematically increase groundwater pumping to offset surface water deficits, this would perfectly mask the operational buffering effect of the reservoir in the ET and cropland data. The authors acknowledge this limitation and mention the DGA well-level network. Given the high-impact nature of the claims, the authors should utilize the available DGA well hydrograph data to test whether differential groundwater drawdown occurs between the treated and control basins, thereby bounding this critical unobserved confounder. 

2. Unbalanced baseline aridity confound The study relies on a doublyrobust outcome model to adjust for a massive baseline aridity gap (P/PET 0.26 vs 0.80) between treated and control basins because exact matching collapses the effective sample size. Extrapolating across such a severe regime gap using a parametric model risks masking true effects, especially for land-use and evapotranspiration outcomes that are highly non-linear with respect to aridity. While the upstream placebo robustly addresses this for streamflow, the cross-sectional ATTs for cropland expansion and ET buffering remain highly vulnerable to this confound. The authors should restrict the primary ATT estimates to the aridity-overlap subset rather than relying on parametric extrapolation across disjoint regimes, or explicitly downgrade the confidence of the demand-side claims compared to the streamflow claims. 

3. Confounded reservoir storage decline The manuscript attributes the observed downward drift in reservoir storage to a climate-driven falling water supply, comparing it to declines in unregulated streamflow. However, without analyzing naturalized inflows or operational release records, this decline is perfectly confounded with potential increases in unrecorded upstream extractions or systematic changes in reservoir management and release schedules over the 20-year period. The authors should soften the causal language attributing storage declines strictly to climate-driven supply reductions, or incorporate a water balance analysis for the reservoirs to partition the decline between reduced inflow and altered outflows/ extractions. 

4. Unaddressed reservoir use heterogeneity The manuscript treats reservoir presence as a uniform basin-level intervention, yet reservoir operating rules and their socio-hydrological impacts vary significantly by primary use (e.g., irrigation, hydropower, multi-purpose). While the national dam registry provides data on reservoir use, the analysis does not appear to stratify or test for heterogeneous effects across these types. Because hydropower reservoirs are not primarily managed to buffer agricultural or consumptive drought, including them alongside irrigation reservoirs could dilute a potential buffering signal. The authors should contextualize these findings within the broader literature on reservoir typology and discuss whether the observed null effect is consistent across different reservoir purposes, or if sample size limitations preclude this stratification. 

5. Unquantified inter-basin transfer spillovers The manuscript acknowledges that inter-basin transfers could violate spatial independence and bias the matched contrast toward the null. Given that such transfers are a comerstone of Chile's drought response strategy, this represents a substantial limitation that warrants deeper discussion. If a treated basin exports water to a control basin, or vice versa, the drought transmission slopes of both units are artificially coupled. The authors should expand on how prevalent these transfers are among the specific 21 treated and 91 effective control basins used in the final matched sample. Providing context on whether major known transfers overlap with the matched pairs would help clarify the magnitude of this potential SUTVA violation. 

6. Administrative vs. realized water demand The use of the DGA consolidated registry of water-use rights as a direct demand-side outcome captures administrative demand and legal entitlement, but it may not accurately reflect realized water consumption or actual demand expansion during a megadrought. Under the Chilean Water Code, the stock of granted rights often exceeds physical water availability, leading to proportional curtailments in practice. Consequently, a null finding in the accrual of new rights might simply reflect administrative closure of over-allocated basins rather than a lack of underlying demand growth. The authors should explicitly discuss the limitation of using allocated rights as a proxy for realized demand and contextualize how administrative basin closures might influence the interpretation of the supply-demand cycle in this specific governance regime. 

7. whole-basin ET pre-trend p-value The text states the pre-trend failure p- value for the whole-basin ET is 3 x 10-5. However, the Table 1 caption states that "Whole-basin ET fails its event-study pre-trend test (p approximately 1e-10)". These two p-values for the same test contradict each other.
