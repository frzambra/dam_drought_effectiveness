## Reviewer Comments 

1. Unmeasured historical induced demand The authors conclude that reservoirs "mark, not make" agricultural vulnerability because cropland area and water rights did not differentially expand during the 2000-2024 study window. However, the manuscript notes that 18 of the 24 reservoirs were commissioned before this period. The observed 4.6-fold crosssectional gap in cropland between dammed and control basins could therefore be the result of historical induced demand that occurred immediately following reservoir construction, reaching a new equilibrium prior to the study window. Because the study design cannot evaluate the longitudinal coevolutionary loop of demand expansion following commissioning, the absolute claim that reservoirs do not "make" vulnerability is not fully supported by the time-invariant treatment data. I recommend revising the language in the abstract and section headers to clarify that reservoirs did not induce additional demand during the recent megadrought, rather than ruling out the induced-demand hypothesis entirely. 

2. Overstated conclusion on storage amplitude The manuscript states that the seasonal amplitude shows no detectable trend and concludes that the storage band "drops without clear narrowing." However, the confidence intervals for this trend are described as "wide," indicating that the analysis is underpowered to detect changes in refill amplitude. Failing to reject the null hypothesis due to wide confidence intervals does not logically support the affirmative conclusion that the band drops without narrowing. I recommend softening the claims regarding the preservation of the refill amplitude and explicitly stating that the data are insufficient to determine whether the buffering capacity (amplitude) has degraded over time. 

3. ET measurement bias toward the null The authors appropriately acknowledge that the MOD16 product underestimates evapotranspiration over irrigated land, which biases the orchard-ET gap toward zero. However, because the study relies heavily on equivalence testing and bounding null effects to refute the induced-demand hypothesis, using an outcome measure with a known bias toward the null is highly problematic. It artificially inflates the likelihood of finding no effect. While the authors state a thermal ET product would be needed to fully exclude an induced-demand signal, they should either provide a sensitivity analysis using a higher-resolution thermal ET product (e.g., Landsat or ECOSTRESS) for a representative subset of basins, or substantially temper the claims regarding the absence of an induced-demand effect in the abstract and discussion, explicitly noting that the ET null may be an artifact of the dataset's limitations. 

4. Equivalence margin lacks physical volume context The authors define the equivalence margin for the TOST as +25% of the baseline transmission slope, justified by the inability of this effect size to shift a basin across a 0.5-unit standardized drought severity category. However, as the authors acknowledge, a 25% change in the SSI slope could represent a substantial absolute volume of water in large basins. Because the socio-hydrological impacts of reservoirs (e.g., agricultural resilience, urban supply) depend on physical water volumes rather than standardized indices, anchoring the equivalence margin solely to discrete SSI categories risks declaring practically meaningful volumetric effects as "negligible." To ensure the equivalence bounds are robustly interpreted, the authors should translate the 25% SSI slope margin into an approximate absolute streamflow volume (e.g., in cubic meters or mm/year) for a representative treated basin, and discuss whether this physical volume is genuinely negligible for local water management. 

- o. Generalizability across governance regimes The manuscript effectively contextualizes the findings within Chile's unique 1981 Water Code, which establishes perpetual private water rights. However, to maximize the impact of this study for a global audience, the discussion should briefly address how these findings might generalize to regions with fundamentally different water governance frameworks. For instance, the authors should discuss whether the observed lack of operational buffering and the manifestation of the reservoir effect would be expected to persist in systems with prior appropriation doctrines or highly regulated state-owned allocations, thereby expanding on how governance structures mediate the socio-hydrological supply-demand cycle. 

6. Clarification of streamflow decline source The manuscript reports a 2.9% per year decline in unregulated streamflow in undammed control basins. It is slightly ambiguous whether this specific statistic is a novel calculation derived entirely from the authors' current dataset or if it is corroborated by prior regional hydrological assessments of the megadrought. If this is a primary result of the current analysis, it should be explicitly stated as such; if it aligns with existing literature on Chilean streamflow trends during the megadrought, appropriate citations should be provided to situate this magnitude of decline within the broader hydrological consensus. 

7. Contextualizing the socio-hydrological debate The manuscript provides a rigorous empirical test of the reservoir effect, a concept heavily theorized in socio-hydrology. While the citations to Di Baldassarre et al. and Kellner are appropriate, the discussion could more deeply situate the findings within the broader debate on human-water co-evolution. Specifically, the authors should briefly discuss how their empirical null results challenge or refine the theoretical models of the supply-demand cycle that currently dominate the literature, explicitly connecting the causal inference results back to the specific mechanisms proposed in these theoretical frameworks. 

8. Inconsistent minimum detectable effect bound The manuscript inconsistently reports the upper bound of the minimum detectable effect for streamflow buffering. In two places (lines 84 and 195-196), the text states the design can detect or excludes buffering larger than '46 to 58%' of baseline transmission. However, on line 103, it states the design rules out buffering larger than '46 to 59%'. A reader could draw incompatible conclusions about the exact upper bound of this range.
