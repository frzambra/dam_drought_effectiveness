## Reviewer Comments 

1. Snowpack buffering confounds the upstream placebo 

The study relies heavily on the upstream versus downstream placebo test to rule out a reservoir buffering effect, arguing that because upstream reaches also show flattened transmission slopes, the downstream buffering is merely an artifact of location. However, as acknowledged in the text, upstream Andean headwaters are snow-dominated. Snowpack acts as a natural reservoir that buffers streamflow against meteorological drought. If the upstream reach is buffered by snowmelt and the downstream reach is buffered by the dam, comparing their transmission slopes may yield a null difference despite the dam actively providing a buffering effect that replaces the lost snowpack signal at lower elevations. To logically support the claim that the dam provides no buffering, the analysis must account for these differential buffering mechanisms. I recommend incorporating a snow metric (e.g., Snow Water Equivalent or MODIS fractional snow cover) into the transmission model to isolate the snow-buffering effect from the baseline upstream transmission slope, or restricting the placebo test to nonsnow-dominated catchments. 

2. Administrative closure confounds demand outcomes 

The conclusion that reservoirs do not induce demand relies on the observation that water rights and cropland did not expand differentially in dammed basins. However, if these arid, dammed basins were already administratively closed to new water rights prior to or during the megadrought due to over-allocation, a null result in new rights accrual is a forced artifact of policy, not a reflection of socio-hydrological dynamics. Furthermore, if a basin is already at maximum physical and legal capacity, the counterfactual without a reservoir might be a severe collapse in cropland rather than stagnation. The current design tests for expansion but does not adequately evaluate whether the reservoir prevented a collapse relative to matched controls that were not operating at maximum capacity. The authors should explicitly test whether the baseline utilization rate (current allocations vs. total renewable water) differs between treated and control basins, and discuss how ceiling effects limit the interpretation of the demand-side null. 

3. Unresolved aridity confounding in matched controls 

The study acknowledges a severe baseline aridity mismatch between dammed (median P/PET 0.26) and undammed (0.80) basins. Relying on a linear parametric adjustment in the doubly-robust outcome model to bridge such a massive regime gap is highly susceptible to model misspecification. While the authors perform a robustness check on an aridity-overlap subset, the fundamental ecological and hydrological differences between hyper-arid/ arid basins and humid basins cannot be fully resolved by linear or quadratic covariates. The response of a basin with a P/PET of 0.26 to a 12-month SPEI deficit is likely non-linearly distinct from a basin at 0.80. I suggest presenting the exact-matched analysis restricted strictly to the ESS=19 subset where aridity is hard-balanced as a primary sensitivity analysis, rather than relying on parametric extrapolation across distinct climate regimes to maintain statistical power. 

4. Inappropriate ET product for irrigated arid lands 

The use of MOD16 to evaluate evapotranspiration in heavily irrigated arid orchards is fundamentally flawed for this specific application, as the authors acknowledge. Because MOD16 relies on meteorological inputs rather than thermal infrared signatures of actual surface cooling, it systematically misses the exact anthropogenic irrigation signal (decoupling of soil moisture from precipitation) that the study aims to measure. Dismissing a potential positive induced-demand signal as a mere artifact of MOD16 underestimation weakens the logical rigor of the ET analysis. To make any credible claims regarding ET buffering or induced demand, I recommend replacing or supplementing the MOD16 data with a thermal-based ET product (such as Landsat-based SSEBop, METRIC, or ECOSTRESS data) that directly captures consumptive water use in irrigated arid environments regardless of local precipitation deficits. 

5. Absence of evidence in pre-trend tests 

The authors interpret a high p- value (p = 0.65) from a linear trend-slope test as evidence that the differential pre-trend is "flat." However, a high p-value only indicates a failure to reject the null hypothesis of no difference, which may simply result from low statistical power (as suggested by the wide confidence intervals prior to 2010 in Figure 4b). Given that the authors rigorously apply equivalence testing and minimum detectable effect (MDE) frameworks to their main null results, they should apply a similar standard to the pre-trend analysis. The authors should report the MDE or confidence intervals for the pre-2010 trend slope to clarify whether the design is sufficiently powered to rule out meaningful pre-existing divergent trends. 

6. Post-treatment bias in baseline aridity 

The authors use the 1991—2020 WMO normal to calculate baseline aridity for matching and covariate adjustment. However, the manuscript defines the megadrought as beginning in 2010. Consequently, 11 years of the baseline window overlap with the drought period. If reservoirs locally influence evapotranspiration (PET) during drought conditions—for instance, through sustained irrigation and land-atmosphere feedbacks—then aridity during the 2010-2020 period is partially an outcome of the treatment. Matching on a potentially posttreatment variable can introduce bias. To ensure the matching relies strictly on pre-existing conditions, the authors should calculate baseline aridity using a strictly pre-drought climatological window (e.g., 1980—2009) or demonstrate that P/PET is invariant to reservoir presence during the drought. 

7. Non-stationary snowmelt in upstream placebo 

The use of upstream unregulated reaches as a within-basin placebo is a strong design element, but the assumption that snowmelt acts as a static natural buffer may be compromised by climate change. The Andes have experienced significant warming and a rising zero-degree isotherm during the megadrought, leading to non-stationary snowpack dynamics and earlier melt timing. If the upstream natural buffer is actively degrading over the 2005-2024 study period, its transmission slope will dynamically steepen. Comparing a degrading downstream reservoir buffer to a degrading upstream snow buffer could artificially produce a null difference. The manuscript should contextualize this non-stationarity using recent literature on Andean snow hydrology and discuss how parallel degradation of natural and artificial buffers might influence the placebo test's validity. 

8. Representativeness of groundwater network 

The bounding of the groundwater substitution confounder relies on depth-to-water trends from 213 DGA wells across 26 matched basins. While the median drawdown rates are statistically indistinguishable, the manuscript lacks context regarding the spatial representativeness of this well network. In heavily managed agricultural basins, pumping is often highly localized around specific high-value crop footprints. If the monitored wells are situated outside these intensive extraction zones or in different aquifer strata, the basin-level median may fail to capture the localized compensatory pumping that sustains evapotranspiration during surface water deficits. The authors should discuss the spatial overlap between the well network and irrigated land, and acknowledge the limitations of using basin-wide medians to rule out localized groundwater substitution. 

9. Oversimplification of inter-basin transfers 

The assertion that unmapped inter-basin transfers strictly bias the results toward the null assumes a unidirectional flow of benefits (from dammed to control basins). However, complex water conveyance systems, such as the Paloma reservoir complex mentioned in the text, often redistribute water dynamically based on seasonal scarcity, potentially moving water into dammed basins from adjacent catchments to sustain high-value agriculture. If a treated basin receives imported water during peak drought, this would artificially flatten its transmission slope, masking underlying vulnerability. A more nuanced discussion of the bidirectional nature of these transfers and their potential to both inflate or obscure the reservoir effect is necessary for a complete interpretation of the bounded null. 

10. Cropland-area expansion estimand contradicts transmission slope description 

Section 2.5 claims that the 'cropland-area expansion’ outcome applies the 'Forcing-conditioned transmission slope’ machinery, which computes a slope with respect to SPEI. However, Table 1 lists its estimand as 'ha km/-2 added' (a simple change in levels), and Line 185 defines it as ‘the change in MapBiomas cropland area per basin over the window’, contradicting the claim that it is a transmission slope. 

11. Unexplained drop in control basin sample size

Section 2.2 states that removing contaminated units leaves '398 clean controls’ (Line 89). However, Section 2.4 states the matching procedure uses '244 in-window controls' (Line 150), and subsequent sections and figures (e.g., Figure 4 caption) refer to 244 controls. The manuscript does not explain why 154 clean controls were excluded from the matching pool. 12. Inconsistent baseline transmission slope value In Section 2.5, the manuscript states the baseline transmission slope is 0.585 (Line 208). However, in Section 3.2, when calculating the relative size of the residual buffering, it refers to 'the 0.53 baseline transmission slope' (Line 301). It is unclear why the baseline transmission slope changes between these two sections.
