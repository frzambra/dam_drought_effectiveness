## Reviewer Comments 

1. Geomorphology confounds upstream placebo The within-basin placebo relies on comparing unregulated upstream reaches to regulated downstream reaches, using elevation as a linear proxy to adjust for differences. However, channel geomorphology and alluvial storage are perfectly confounded with this upstream/downstream comparison. Andean upstream reaches are typically steep and bedrock-controlled, whereas downstream reaches occupy lower-gradient alluvial valleys with significant surfacegroundwater exchange that naturally buffers streamflow. Because the transition from bedrock to alluvial channels is often a non-linear stepchange at the mountain front, a linear adjustment based on elevation among control gauges likely fails to capture the true magnitude of natural alluvial buffering. Consequently, it is impossible to determine whether the observed downstream transmission slope reflects a lack of reservoir buffering or is masked by fundamental differences in geomorphological buffering capacity. The authors should incorporate a direct spatial metric of alluvial valley width or aquifer storage capacity rather than relying solely on elevation. 

2. Spatial dependence in balancing weights The manuscript appropriately addresses spatial autocorrelation in the outcome analysis by utilizing a spatially-restricted watershed-block permutation test for inference. However, the entropy balancing procedure used to construct the matched control weights assumes independent observations. If the covariates used for matching (e.g., elevation, area, aridity) are also spatially autocorrelated, the resulting balancing weights may be inefficient or fail to adequately balance the spatial structure of the confounders. The authors should clarify whether the entropy balancing procedure accounts for spatial dependence (e.g., by incorporating spatial coordinates or watershed blocks into the balancing constraints) or discuss the potential limitations of assuming independence during the weighting phase. 

3. Post-hoc exclusion of outcomes The authors relegate the evapotranspiration (ET) outcomes to the supplement, citing failed pretrends for whole-basin ET and a known bias toward zero in the MOD16 product for the orchard stratum. While the justification regarding MOD16's limitations is plausible, deciding to disqualify an outcome metric after observing a null result introduces a risk of analytical bias. The authors should clarify whether the limitations of the MOD16 dataset over irrigated lands were identified a priori or only after the analysis yielded null results. If this was a post-hoc decision, the manuscript should transparently discuss this as a limitation and integrate the ET findings into the main text with appropriate caveats, rather than dismissing the null finding entirely based on data quality concerns raised after the fact. 

4. Misleading confidence intervals The manuscript acknowledges that cluster-robust standard errors over-reject with only ~21 treated clusters, prompting the use of randomization inference for hypothesis testing. However, Figure 4b displays 95% confidence intervals based on these same cluster-robust standard errors (+/- 1.96 SE). Because these standard errors are known to be downwardly biased in this small-cluster setting, the visual representation of uncertainty in the event study is artificially narrow. The authors should construct and display confidence intervals derived from the permutation distribution or use a small-sample correction for the clusterrobust standard errors (e.g., wild cluster bootstrap) to ensure the visual evidence aligns with the stated inferential limitations. 

5. Misalignment of timescale with dam design The study evaluates reservoir buffering at a 12-month accumulation scale to assess multi-year drought vulnerability. However, if the primary engineering purpose of the irrigation reservoirs in the treated sample is intra-annual (seasonal) regulation rather than multi-year carryover, the absence of a 12-month buffering effect is a deterministic outcome of their design capacity rather than a novel sociohydrological finding. The manuscript lacks literature context regarding the actual design capacities and intended operational timescales of these specific dams. The authors must clarify whether these reservoirs were built with the capacity for multi-year storage; if they were not, the conclusion that they fail to buffer multi-year drought tests a function they were never designed to perform. 

6. Incomplete capture of agricultural demand The conclusion that reservoirs do not induce demand during the megadrought relies heavily on total cropland area and water rights registries. However, as acknowledged, these metrics fail to capture agricultural intensification or transitions to high-water-demand export crops, which are well-documented adaptations in central Chile. Because the study cannot measure changes in crop water requirements within existing footprints, the claim that reservoirs "neither added demand nor buffering" may prematurely dismiss demand-side expansion. The authors should explicitly discuss how crop transitions could mask induced demand and contextualize this gap within the existing literature on Chile's agricultural export shifts during the megadrought.
