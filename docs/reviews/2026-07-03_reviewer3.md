## Reviewer Comments

1. Masking of short-term buffering dynamics

The study uses a single linear transmission slope (SPEI-12 to SSI-12) over the entire 2005–2024 period to conclude that reservoirs do not buffer drought. However, this time-averaged approach may mask temporal dynamics, particularly the depletion of storage. A reservoir with 1-2 years of capacity may successfully buffer the first few years of a megadrought but fail once depleted. By calculating a single slope across a 15-year megadrought, early-stage buffering could be statistically washed out by late-stage vulnerability. I recommend splitting the drought period into early (e.g., 2010–2014) and late (e.g., 2015–2024) phases, or using a rolling-window regression, to determine if the upstream/downstream placebo holds during the initial onset of the drought before storage was severely compromised.

2. Parametric extrapolation across aridity gap

The study relies heavily on a parametric outcome model to adjust for a residual aridity imbalance (SMD 0.17) because the treated basins are systematically more arid than the controls. While the authors note that a hard-balanced match (ESS = 19) yields similar null results, an effective sample size of 19 is extremely low for a national-scale analysis, raising concerns about statistical power. Because aridity is perfectly confounded with reservoir siting, relying on a linear log-aridity adjustment to extrapolate across this regime gap risks masking true effects. The authors should provide a non-parametric overlap plot of the aridity distributions for treated and control basins to demonstrate that the doubly-robust model is interpolating within a region of common support rather than extrapolating outside of it.

3. Underpowered dynamic event study

The conclusion that cropland trajectories do not diverge after the onset of the megadrought relies on a failure to reject the null hypothesis in the dynamic event study (Figure 4b). However, the randomization-inference null envelope (grey band) expands massively after 2015, indicating a severe lack of statistical power in the later years of the panel. Concluding that reservoirs added no vulnerability based on a p-value of 0.81 is a logical leap when the confidence intervals are wide enough to accommodate substantial divergence. The language should be revised to explicitly state that the post-2015 data is too noisy to rule out divergence, rather than framing the high p-value as definitive confirmation of parallel trajectories.

4. Groundwater masking of induced demand

The acknowledgment that unmonitored groundwater drawdown could sustain the agricultural footprint during the megadrought is a critical limitation that complicates the interpretation of the supply-demand cycle. If reservoirs historically induced agricultural expansion that is now being maintained by unsustainable groundwater pumping, the reservoir effect is not absent; it has merely shifted to a different resource pool. The discussion should more explicitly contextualize how this potential resource substitution aligns with or challenges the conclusion that reservoirs do not amplify drought vulnerability.

5. Disconnect between paper rights and use

The use of the DGA registry's allocated rights as a proxy for water demand assumes a reliable relationship between granted rights and actual water consumption. In many Chilean basins, water rights are over-allocated, and the paper rights recorded in the registry may not reflect actual wet water usage, especially during a severe drought when physical availability is the binding constraint. The manuscript would benefit from a discussion situating this metric within the broader literature on Chile's water market, acknowledging how the hoarding or non-use of paper rights might dampen the observed demand-side response.

6. Unaccounted land-cover confounding in placebo

While the authors rigorously adjust for elevation differences between upstream and downstream gauges to validate the placebo, they do not account for potential differences in land cover or localized unregulated diversions. Upstream reaches in Andean catchments often feature distinct vegetation dynamics and lower anthropogenic modification compared to downstream valleys. The authors should discuss how differences in land cover or unrecorded upstream extractions might influence the SPEI-to-SSI transmission slope, potentially confounding the upstream placebo test.
