## Fatal Flaws 

## |. Standardization of streamflow mathematically erases reservoir 

buffering The conclusion that reservoirs do not buffer streamflow drought relies on comparing the transmission slope of meteorological drought (SPEI-12) to hydrological drought (SSI-12) between upstream (unregulated) and downstream (regulated) gauges. However, the Standardized Streamflow Index (SSI) normalizes streamflow relative to a gauge's own historical distribution, forcing the variance to 1. Because the downstream gauges are standardized on the post-dam (2000-2020) regulated flow regime, the variance-reducing effect of the reservoir (clipping peaks and raising troughs) is mathematically divided out during the SSI calculation. Consequently, an SSI of -1.0 downstream represents a much smaller absolute volumetric deficit than an SSI of -1.0 upstream. By regressing a standardized flow index on a standardized precipitation index, the analysis only tests whether the reservoir alters the correlation between precipitation and flow percentiles, completely masking the absolute volumetric buffering the dam provides. This invalidates the primary evidence for the claim that dams do not buffer drought. 

## Reviewer Comments 

1. Parametric extrapolation across aridity regimes The median P/PET difference (0.26 vs 0.80) represents fundamentally different hydrological regimes (arid vs. humid/Mediterranean). Relying on a linear log-aridity adjustment in a doubly-robust model to bridge this massive gap assumes that the drought transmission elasticity scales smoothly and parametrically across completely different climate zones. While the authors mention an "aridity-overlap subset," the primary headline estimates rely on this extrapolation. The authors should explicitly demonstrate that the SPEI-toSSI relationship holds its functional form across this specific P/PET gradient in the undammed control basins to justify this parametric adjustment, or elevate the non-parametric overlap-subset analysis to the main text. 

2. Overstated causal claims from bounded nulls The abstract definitively claims that reservoirs "added none" (no vulnerability or buffering) through the megadrought. However, the text later clarifies that the design only excludes streamflow buffering above 46 to 59% of baseline. Concluding an absolute zero effect from a bounded null with a ~50% margin of baseline is a logical overstatement, as effects within this wide margin could still be volumetrically and operationally significant. The language in the abstract and introduction should be softened to accurately reflect that operational buffering or induced demand could not be detected beyond these specific volumetric bounds, rather than claiming they do not exist at all. 

3. Non-linear snowmelt confounding in elevation placebo The upstream placebo relies on multiplying an elevation gap bya linear control sensitivity to elevation. However, a 540-meter elevation difference in the Andes often crosses the snowline, fundamentally shifting the basin from a rainfalldominated to a snowmelt-dominated regime. Snowpack introduces interseasonal memory that alters the SPEI-12 to SSI-12 transmission slope in ways that are unlikely to be strictly linear with elevation. Although the authors attempt a forcing-by-snowpack adjustment, using a linear elevation sensitivity to mathematically "over-explain" the difference risks masking non-linear hydrological buffering provided by snowpack. The authors should validate the linearity of the elevation-to-transmission relationship specifically across the snowline transition in their control basins. 

4. Absolute versus relative cropland expansion metrics The massive baseline difference in cropland area (11.1% vs 2.4%) means that the matched controls are fundamentally different in their land-use intensity. Evaluating cropland-area expansion as an absolute level-change outcome (ha km-2 added) between these two vastly different baselines is problematic. A 1% absolute increase in basin area represents a ~9% relative expansion for dammed basins but a ~40% relative expansion for controls. The parallel trends assumption in absolute terms may not hold if agricultural expansion scales proportionally to existing infrastructure. The authors should justify why absolute expansion is the appropriate metric rather than relative expansion, and consider providing a relative growth rate comparison to ensure the null result is not an artifact of the metric chosen. 

5. Missing watershed-grain spillover results The manuscript states that residual adjacency and shared-aquifer spillovers are "checked at watershed grain" to address potential SUTVA violations. However, the quantitative results of these watershed-level checks are not reported in the main text or explicitly referenced in the supplementary material. Because SUTVA is a foundational assumption for the causal estimators used (e.g., doubly-robust matched ATT), the outcome of this robustness check is critical for validating the sub-watershed estimates. Please include the results of the watershed-grain analysis to confirm whether spatial spillovers materially bias the primary findings. 

6. Overstated pre-trend confirmation The authors conclude that the pretreatment trajectories for cropland area evolve in parallel, supporting the validity of the difference-in-differences design. However, the minimum detectable effect for this pre-trend test is extremely large, requiring a divergence equal to half the control cropland level to be detected. Given this low statistical power, the failure to reject the null hypothesis of no pretrend does not strongly confirm that the trends are parallel; it only confirms that catastrophic divergence is absent. The language should be tempered to reflect that the parallel trends assumption remains a source of uncertainty, as biologically or economically meaningful pre-treatment divergences cannot be statistically ruled out by the current sample size. 

7. Missing positive control parameters The authors use a positive control by injecting a "known buffering slope" to demonstrate that their design is not underpowered. However, the methodological details of this injection are absent from the text. It is unclear what magnitude of buffering was injected, whether the injection was additive or proportional, and how the baseline variance was preserved during this synthetic modification. To ensure reproducibility and allow readers to evaluate the validity of this power check, please provide the specific computational parameters and equations used to generate the positive control data. 

8. Temporal scope of induced demand The authors correctly acknowledge that their study window (2005-2024) primarily captures the megadrought era, meaning the 4.6-fold cropland gap likely represents legacy demand induced prior to the study period. However, the socio-hydrological supplydemand cycle posits that demand expands during periods of perceived water abundance (post-construction) and subsequently exacerbates vulnerability when a severe drought eventually occurs. Because the study design only observes the drought period itself—a time when further agricultural expansion is naturally constrained by acute water scarcity—the finding that reservoirs added no vulnerability through the megadrought does not fully test the supply-demand cycle. The manuscript would benefit from a more nuanced discussion contextualizing how the temporal limitations of the dataset interact with the theoretical phases of the reservoir effect, ensuring the conclusions do not overstate the null finding regarding induced demand. 

9. Inclusion of disqualified ET data The manuscript presents evapotranspiration (ET) results in the main text, Table 1, and Figure 3, only to explicitly disqualify them from the evidence base due to pre-trend failures and known biases in the MOD16 product over irrigated lands. While transparency is commendable, featuring fundamentally flawed analyses in the primary figures and tables dilutes the paper's rigorous findings. If the MOD16 data cannot reliably capture consumptive water use in this context, the authors should either relegate this analysis to the supplementary materials with a clear methodological caveat or replace it with a higher-resolution thermal ET product to provide a complete and valid test of the consumptive use pathway. 

10. Generalizability to low-gradient basins The use of the upstream/ downstream placebo is a highly effective causal inference strategy for this study. However, the authors briefly note that this approach is most applicable to short, steep catchments and may be blurred in large, lowgradient basins due to transit and floodplain storage. Given that many of the world's most contested and heavily regulated river basins are large and lowgradient, the authors should expand on this limitation in the discussion. Specifically, they should propose coherent future directions or methodological adaptations that could allow this rigorous causal framework to be applied to broader, more diverse geomorphological contexts, thereby enhancing the global relevance of the study's methodological contributions. 11. Cross-sectional ATTs count mismatch The Methods section explicitly states there are 'two cross-sectional ATTs in Table 1' and lists them as (i) Cropland-area expansion and (ii) Reservoir ET buffering. However, Table 1 actually lists three cross-sectional matched ATTs, including 'Water-rights accrual’ as the third.
