# Fatal Flaws

1. Underpowered design overstates null

The headline claim asserts that reservoirs show no measurable operational buffer against drought. However, the paired placebo analysis shows that the downstream transmission slope is flatter than the upstream slope (-0.16 vs. -0.20), which is directionally consistent with operational buffering. The authors dismiss this difference because it is not statistically significant, but the paired placebo design is highly underpowered, with a minimum detectable effect (MDE) of 0.28 SSI units (about 52% of baseline). This means the design is physically incapable of detecting moderate but water-volumetrically significant buffering effects. Concluding that reservoirs show 'no measurable buffer' when the study lacks the statistical power to detect anything short of a massive, near-complete elimination of drought transmission is an overstatement of the null. The headline and major claims should be qualified to clarify that only very large operational buffering effects are ruled out, and moderate buffering remains entirely consistent with the data.


# Reviewer Comments

1. Inconsistent sign in elevation-driven slope prediction

The explanation of the elevation gradient on page 15 contains a contradiction in the direction of the predicted slope change. Since the transmission slope from SPEI-12 to SSI-12 is positive (as shown in Figure 2a), a negative coefficient of -0.21 per km means the slope becomes less positive (flatter) at higher elevations. Therefore, the 0.54 km elevation gap should predict a flatter (less positive) upstream slope, rather than a 'steeper (more negative)' one. Additionally, if the control slope flattens (decreases) by 0.11 units upstream, the baseline is lower, which would mathematically predict a less negative (closer to zero) differential slope (treatment effect) upstream, not a more negative one. The authors should revise this terminology to align with the positive sign of the transmission slope and the flattening effect of elevation.

2. Pooling seasonal and carryover reservoirs dilutes effects

The authors pool seasonal reservoirs (with a median capacity of only 0.3 years of downstream flow) with carryover reservoirs in a 12-month accumulation analysis. Seasonal reservoirs lack the physical capacity to carry water over across years, meaning they are physically incapable of buffering multi-year drought. Consequently, analyzing their effect on a 12-month accumulation timescale is highly likely to yield a null result by design, as seasonal redistribution of flow does not alter 12-month water volumes. Pooling these seasonal reservoirs with the few actual carryover reservoirs dilutes any potential multi-year buffering effect that larger reservoirs might possess. The authors should disaggregate the analysis by reservoir storage capacity (e.g., capacity-to-inflow ratio) to show whether larger carryover reservoirs exhibit operational buffering, rather than drawing a sweeping conclusion about all reservoirs from a sample dominated by seasonal ones.

3. Baseline cropland imbalance invalidates controls

The matched control basins have a baseline cropland area of only 2.4% of the basin area, compared to 10.8% in the dammed basins (a 4.5-fold difference). This massive baseline imbalance indicates that the control basins are not valid counterfactuals for the dammed basins. Basins with highly developed agricultural sectors (10.8% cropland) face different economic constraints, infrastructure networks, and institutional water demands than basins with minimal agriculture (2.4% cropland). Since baseline cropland was not included as a matching target in the entropy balancing procedure, the parallel trends assumption in the DiD analysis is highly suspect, as the drivers of cropland dynamics in highly agricultural basins are likely different from those in undeveloped basins. The authors should include baseline cropland area as a matching covariate to construct a more comparable control group.

4. Linear trend mischaracterizes step-change

Using a linear OLS model over 2005-2024 mischaracterizes the temporal dynamics of reservoir storage, which are dominated by a step-change in 2010 at the onset of the megadrought rather than a continuous downward drift. If the decline is a step-change rather than a continuous drift, the linear OLS model mischaracterizes the temporal dynamics. The authors should test for a step-change in 2010 versus a continuous trend, or report the trend specifically within the megadrought period (2010-2024) to see if storage is indeed continuously drifting downward or has stabilized at a lower equilibrium.

5. Institutional closure guarantees null result

The authors use the accrual of new state-granted water rights from the DGA registry to test for reservoir-induced demand expansion. However, as they acknowledge, many of these basins have been administratively closed to new allocations for decades. In closed basins, the institutional ceiling prevents the state from granting new rights, meaning that any demand expansion or water reallocation must occur through private market transactions, which are not recorded in the DGA registry. Consequently, using state-granted rights accrual as a metric is highly flawed, as a null result is institutionally guaranteed by the basin closure rather than reflecting a true lack of demand expansion. This makes the water-rights accrual analysis a poor operationalization of demand expansion, and the authors should temper their conclusions or rely solely on physical metrics like crop type transitions or high-resolution actual evapotranspiration.

6. Incorrect catchment size proxy

The authors state that 'upstream gauges sit in units of essentially identical area to downstream gauges (mean 3,156 versus 3,071 km2)' to rule out catchment size effects. However, the 'unit area' refers only to the local DGA sub-watershed polygon in which the gauge is located, not the actual cumulative contributing catchment area of the gauge. By definition, a downstream gauge on the same river must have a larger cumulative contributing area than the upstream gauge. Using the local polygon area as a proxy for catchment size is hydrologically incorrect, as streamflow is a function of cumulative upstream drainage, not the size of the administrative polygon containing the gauge. The authors should calculate and control for the actual cumulative upstream catchment area for each gauge rather than using local polygon areas.

7. Vanishing effect is power-loss artifact

The authors argue that hard-balancing aridity makes the apparent buffering vanish because the ITT effect becomes -0.02 with a p-value > 0.85. However, this matching procedure collapses the effective control sample size (ESS) to just 19. When the ESS is so small, the standard errors of the estimator inflate dramatically, which naturally drives p-values toward non-significance. If the point estimate itself did not shift substantially compared to the primary model, then the 'vanishing' of the effect is a statistical artifact of inflated variance rather than evidence of confounding. The authors should clarify whether the change in significance is driven by a shift in the point estimate or by the loss of statistical power, and they should report the standard errors for both specifications to justify their inference.
