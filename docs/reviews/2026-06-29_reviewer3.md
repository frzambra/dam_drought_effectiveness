## Review Report 

Reservoirs mark, but do not make, drought vulnerability: storage buffering reflects siting, not regulation, across Chile 

## Overview 

- Causal Isolation of Reservoir Effects — The authors introduce a novel causal inference framework, utilizing a within-basin upstream/downstream placebo and forcing-conditioned difference-in-differences, to successfully isolate the operational effects of reservoirs from the confounding effects of their geographic siting. 

   - Re-evaluating Drought Buffering — The study demonstrates that the apparent drought-buffering effect of reservoirs in Chile is an artifact of their placement in arid regions, revealing that dams do not operationally mitigate or amplify drought impacts once pre-existing siting vulnerabilities are accounted for. 

## Fatal Flaws 

No fatal methodological flaws were identified.

## Reviewer Comments 

|. Unutilized drawdown proxy for mechanism The authors propose an alternative interpretation where damming recomposes the dependence structure of drought impacts, but state they cannot adjudicate this without reservoir release records. However, they mention that a "drawdown proxy is on disk." Given that this regime shift would fundamentally alter the interpretation of the null effect, the authors should utilize this available drawdown proxy to conduct a preliminary test of the dependence-shift hypothesis. If the proxy is insufficient for this purpose, the text should be clarified to explain exactly why it cannot be used to evaluate the storage-tooutcome coupling. 2. Unseparated demand-side drawdown The conclusion that the operational case for expanding storage is weak relies on the assumption that the downward drift in the storage band is primarily driven bya falling supply ceiling rather than increased demand-side drawdown. While the authors acknowledge this limitation, they subsequently lean on the supply-side interpretation to dismiss the operational value of new storage. Given the socio-hydrological feedbacks introduced earlier in the manuscript, an increase in consumptive demand could equally explain the downward shift without narrowing the amplitude. To logically support the claim that supply is the binding constraint, the authors should integrate their existing evapotranspiration (ET) or irrigated area data as a proxy for demand trends over the same period, or revise the text to explicitly state that demandinduced depletion cannot be ruled out as a driver of reservoir failure. 

3. Arbitrary equivalence testing threshold The authors define the negligible region for the two one-sided test (TOST) as +25% of the baseline transmission slope. However, there is no justification provided for why 25% constitutes a biologically, hydrologically, or operationally "negligible" effect in this context. Because the outcome of equivalence testing is highly sensitive to the chosen bounds, this threshold must be grounded in prior literature, policy standards, or physical constraints rather than selected arbitrarily. Please provide a clear justification for the +25% threshold. 

4. Unspecified error bars in figures The manuscript includes several figures with shaded regions or error bars where the statistical meaning is not explicitly defined in the captions. For example, Figure 1b contains a shaded region around the event study estimates, Figure 3a includes shaded bands around the regression lines, and Figure 4b displays error bars for the trend slopes. It is unclear whether these represent 95% confidence intervals, 90% confidence intervals, or standard errors. Please update all figure captions to explicitly state the measure of uncertainty being visualized. 

- o- Missing code and data repository Although the manuscript details the software environment, packages, and specific script names used in the analysis pipeline, it lacks a formal Data and Code Availability statement linking to a public repository (such as GitHub, Zenodo, or OSF). For a computational study relying heavily on custom pipelines and specific functional form adjustments, providing the actual code and data is essential for reproducibility. Please provide a persistent link or DOI to the repository containing the pipeline, environment files, and underlying datasets. 

6. Generalizability of governance context The manuscript effectively highlights how Chile's 1981 Water Code and its highly privatized, marketbased water management system incentivize demand expansion and lock in consumptive use. Because this institutional and regulatory context is uniquely extreme compared to many state-managed or dynamically regulated allocation systems globally, it may strongly influence the observed lack of adaptive feedback. The authors should expand their discussion to address how this specific governance structure might limit or influence the generalizability of their findings to basins operating under different water rights regimes. 

7. Justification of 12-month timescale The study relies on 12-month standardized indices (SPEI-12 and SSI-12) to evaluate drought transmission. However, reservoirs are frequently designed and operated to buffer seasonal or short-term meteorological deficits (e.g., 1- to 6-month timescales) rather than multi-year structural aridification. By focusing exclusively on a 12-month aggregation, the analytical model may inherently mask the short-term operational buffering that these structures provide. The authors should explicitly justify the selection of the 12-month timescale and discuss how this choice delineates the boundaries of their conclusions regarding short-term buffering versus long-term vulnerability. 8. Cropland area multiplier contradiction Section 3.1 states that dammed basins have a mean of 11.1% cropland area compared to 2.4% for matched controls. This is a difference of roughly 4.6 times. However, the caption for Figure 1 contradicts this by claiming dammed basins hold "~10x more cropland". 

°- Downstream MDE percentage miscalculation Section 3.4 states that the downstream-only minimum detectable effect (MDE) of 0.31 represents "~59%" of the baseline transmission. However, Table 4 reports the downstream mean transmission baseline as 0.599. An MDE of 0.312 (from Table 2) divided by a baseline of 0.599 is approximately 52%, not 59%.
