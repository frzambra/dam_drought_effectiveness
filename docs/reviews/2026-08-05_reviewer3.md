# Reviewer Comments 

1. Streamflow analysis excludes peak megadrought years The paper's title and abstract claim to evaluate reservoir drought buffering "during Chile's megadrought," which is defined throughout the text as extending from 2010 to 2024. However, the streamflow dataset used for the primary hydrological drought analysis (SSI-12) ends in March 2020. This timeline excludes the most acute phase of the megadrought (the 2019-2022 peak deficits), during which reservoir storage was most severely depleted and operational buffering would have been most critical. Consequently, there is a scope mismatch between the broad temporal claims in the abstract and the actual temporal coverage of the streamflow proof. 

2. Linear model misspecification The relationship between meteorological drought (SPEI) and streamflow drought (SSI) is highly non-linear, particularly under reservoir regulation which operates on threshold-based rules (such as minimum releases or storage-dependent targets). By using a strictly linear regression of SSI-12 on SPEI-12 to estimate the transmission slope, the analysis may fail to capture the buffering effects that occur nonlinearly during extreme dry periods, while being dominated by more frequent near-normal conditions. The authors should test for non-linear transmission or focus specifically on dry-tail deficits (e.g., SPEI < -1.5) to ensure that a non-linear buffering effect is not being masked by the linear specification. 

3. Contradictory use of ET null The authors assert that no operational reservoir effect is detected across land-use, water-rights, or ecological pathways. However, they also state that the evapotranspiration (ET) pathway is disqualified from the evidence because the MODIS MOD16 product has a documented bias toward zero over irrigated land, which artificially forces the ET null. It is a logical contradiction to disqualify the ET data as invalid while simultaneously citing the ET null as evidence supporting the absence of an operational reservoir effect. The authors should remove the ET pathway from their list of null findings or use a valid alternative (such as thermal-based ET) to evaluate this pathway. 

4. Carryover storage conflation The authors conclude that the parallel decline of peak and trough storage levels indicates a supply-side level decline rather than a degradation of buffering capacity (which they associate only with seasonal amplitude). However, for multi-year droughts —the explicit focus of this paper—the primary buffering mechanism is carryover storage (the volume of water held across years). A downward drift in average storage levels directly reduces the volume of carryover water available to buffer subsequent dry years, representing a clear degradation of multi-year buffering capacity. Concluding that buffering capacity has not degraded because seasonal amplitude is unchanged conflates seasonal regulation with multi-year carryover capacity. 

5. Omission of water rights transactions Water-rights accrual is measured using the "grant date" from the DGA registry to build a cumulative stock of allocated rights. However, in Chile's highly allocated water market, many basins have been administratively closed to new allocations for decades, meaning very few new rights are granted by the state. Instead, growing agricultural demand is met through private market transactions (buying, selling, or leasing existing rights), which are registered in local real estate registries rather than the DGA's grant registry. Consequently, the cumulative stock based on original grant dates will show no change, completely missing the reallocation and concentration of water rights below reservoirs. The authors should temper their claim or acknowledge that their data cannot capture market-based water reallocation.
