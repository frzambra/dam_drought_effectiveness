% abstract 

\abstract{Expanding reservoir storage is the reflexive policy response
to intensifying drought, yet whether dams build resilience or merely
mark where vulnerability already concentrates remains untested under
sustained drying. We treat reservoir presence as a basin-level
intervention across central Chile --- 26 monitored reservoirs spanning
the 2010--present megadrought --- and estimate its effect on drought
propagation and water-dependent land use using a national matched design
(entropy balancing within Köppen × aridity strata; 21 of 24 treated
basins retained, effective control sample ≈91), doubly-robust matched
ATT, forcing-conditioned difference-in-differences, and randomization
inference. Across every operational pathway ---
meteorological-to-hydrological drought transmission, evapotranspiration
buffering, and irrigated-area expansion --- the treatment effect
brackets zero once siting is matched out. A within-basin placebo
comparing regulated downstream reaches against physically unregulated
upstream reaches of the same dammed basins (15 of 21 basins) is the
cleanest within-design falsification: the apparent national buffering
signal (slope gap −0.18) is as strong upstream as downstream and
collapses to ≈ −0.03 within climate region (p\_perm = 0.39) --- the
signature of an aridity-gradient confound, not regulation. Refill
efficiency is undiminished while the whole storage band drifts downward,
a pattern consistent with declining water supply rather than a loss of
storage or buffering capacity. Reservoirs in Chile thus appear to mark
pre-existing, siting-determined vulnerability rather than make
resilience; once siting is accounted for, the operational case for
storage expansion as drought protection is not supported by these data.}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Reservoirs are the dominant structural response to drought: when water
shortages inflict economic damage, public pressure tends to drive the
expansion of storage to raise water availability, which in the short
term lowers the frequency, severity and duration of shortage
\citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}. Yet a socio-hydrological perspective
warns that the same infrastructure can be self-defeating over the
planning horizon of a dam (20--30 years) through two coupled feedbacks.
In the \emph{supply--demand cycle}, greater supply enables agricultural,
urban or industrial expansion, so demand grows faster than projected
from socio-economic trends alone and quickly offsets the initial benefit
--- a rebound, or Jevons, effect \citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}. In
the \emph{reservoir effect} --- the drought analogue of the levee effect
--- prolonged reliance on abundant stored water erodes the incentive to
adapt, deepening dependence on infrastructure and amplifying the damage
when a drought finally exceeds storage capacity
\citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}. Global accounting is consistent with
this view: water demand has outpaced storage capacity in recent decades,
and local water histories from Las Vegas, Melbourne and Athens show
consumption rising to meet, and then exceed, each new increment of
supply \citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}, and the storage returns of new
dam construction are themselves diminishing worldwide
\citep{li_diminishing_2023}. Critically, the rebound means the enhanced
damage from over-reliance need not be offset by a lower frequency of
shortage, making the safe-development paradox potentially \emph{more}
dangerous for drought than for floods \citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}.
Whether these feedbacks dominate in practice, or are outweighed by
reservoirs' protective function, remains actively debated
\citep{kellner_controversial_2021}.

The short-term, hydrological side of this ledger is well documented. As
drought propagates from precipitation deficit through soil moisture to
streamflow, modulated by catchment characteristics
\citep{VanLoon2015, wang_propagation_2016, van_loon_hydrological_2015},
comparisons of regulated against naturalized streamflow show that
reservoir operation reduces the duration and magnitude of short-term
(1--3 month) hydrological drought downstream and lengthens the lag
between meteorological and hydrological drought
\citep{wu_non-linear_2017, wu_impacts_2018}, and that reservoir
management can damp rapid drought-to-flood transitions, particularly in
snowmelt-driven catchments \citep{gotte_hydrological_2024}. But the same
regulation can redistribute scarcity in space and time
\citep{van_loon_making_2013}: in the transboundary Tagus, operation of
the Alcántara dam lengthened and intensified droughts \emph{downstream}
of the reservoir relative to the pre-dam period
\citep{lopezmoreno_dam_2009}. The long-term, demand side is far less
constrained empirically. Irrigation modernization that nominally
``saves'' water often expands irrigated area or shifts farmers toward
higher-value, more water-intensive crops --- the efficiency paradox ---
raising consumptive use and basin-scale vulnerability rather than
lowering it \citep{grafton_paradox_2018, jordan_adaptation_2025}.
Whether reservoirs set the same trap remains largely untested.

Chile is an acute testbed. Since 2010 central Chile (30--38° S) has
endured a \emph{megadrought}: an uninterrupted run of dry years with
mean rainfall deficits of 25--45 \%, punctuated by ``hyperdrought''
winters in 2019 and 2021 with deficits exceeding 75 \%
\citep{garreaud_20102015_2017, Garreaud2025}. The event is unprecedented
over the instrumental record and has no clear analogue in millennial
tree-ring reconstructions; roughly one-quarter to one-third of its
intensity is attributable to anthropogenic forcing --- in line with
warming-driven increases in drought risk documented globally
\citep{Trenberth2014, diffenbaugh_anthropogenic_2015} --- the remainder
to Pacific decadal variability and long-term regional drying
\citep{garreaud_20102015_2017, Boisier2018, Montecinos2003, Quintana2012}.
The signal amplifies as it propagates: a \textasciitilde30 \% reduction
in Andean snow-water equivalent \citep{saavedra_changes_2018} --- part
of intensifying snow drought worldwide
\citep{huning_global_2020, cowherd_evolution_2023} --- translates into
streamflow declines of 20--70 \% (locally up to 90 \%), with the
streamflow deficit in arid sectors often twice the rainfall deficit, and
reservoirs and groundwater drawn to historical lows
\citep{garreaud_20102015_2017, AlvarezGarreton2021} --- part of a global
pattern in which most reservoirs now fail to refill under intensifying
drought \citep{wang_reservoir_2024}. Demand, however, has scarcely
relented --- infrastructure and intensive groundwater pumping have held
human withdrawals near pre-drought levels, masking a ``Day Zero'' that
surface water alone could not avert
\citep{AlvarezGarreton2021, Garreaud2025} --- at the cost of aquifer
overexploitation now well documented in central Chile
\citep{Taucare2024, Duran2020, venegas-quinones_development_2024}.
Mounting evidence suggests the region is crossing from episodic drought
into structural aridification, with long-term declines in water supply
--- and rising atmospheric evaporative demand --- driving land-cover
change
\citep{zambrano_drought_2025, Vicente-Serrano2022, Fuentes2021, running_global_2022}.

These dynamics are inseparable from Chile's water governance. The 1981
Water Code, the paradigm case of market-based water management, made
water-use rights tradable, perpetual private property separable from
land \citep{macpherson_evolving_2023, reade_malagueno_libre_2024}.
Rights were granted on the stationarity assumption that historical flows
would persist, producing over-allocated ``paper rights'' in many basins,
while management was delegated largely to private water-user
organizations
\citep{Barria2021, rivera_legal_2016, rivera-vidal_managed_2025, Valdes-Pineda2014}.
Because the state cannot readily curtail existing private rights during
scarcity, the policy reflex has been supply-side: a national programme
of new dams and inter-basin transfers \citep{Barria2021, Garreaud2025}.
The same institutional setting rewarded conversion to high-value,
water-demanding export crops --- orchards and vineyards ---
concentrating water in agro-export firms and locking in consumptive
demand even as supply contracted
\citep{reade_malagueno_libre_2024, webb_water_2021}. Yet most farmers
still meet scarcity with short-term, reactive measures rather than
durable adaptation, leaving long-run vulnerability largely intact
\citep{jordan_adaptation_2025}. Chile thus exhibits, in compressed form,
every ingredient of the reservoir effect: an extreme and persistent
forcing, a supply-side infrastructure response, market incentives for
demand expansion, and weak adaptive feedback.

Despite its prominence, the reservoir effect for drought has been
described far more than it has been \emph{measured}. The framework's
authors note that we lack the datasets and analytical tools to quantify
these social--technical--hydrological feedbacks, and --- above all ---
defensible counterfactuals: what a drought's impact \emph{would have
been} in a basin had the reservoir not been built
\citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}. Existing work compounds the
difficulty. Drought- propagation studies remain mostly temporal, leaving
the spatial footprint of upstream regulation on downstream drought
poorly resolved \citep{schilstra_effects_2024}, and quantitative
comparisons of drought resistance across dams are scarce
\citep{lee_drought_2026}. The core obstacle is identification:
reservoirs are not sited at random but built where irrigation demand and
aridity already concentrate, so dammed basins differ systematically from
undammed ones before any drought strikes. Basin-mean greenness, raw
before--after contrasts, or calendar-time comparisons cannot separate a
genuine reservoir effect from this siting confound.

Here we ask whether reservoirs build resilience or merely postpone and
concentrate vulnerability during prolonged drought. We treat the
presence of a major reservoir as a basin-level intervention and
construct the missing counterfactual by pairing a matched
dammed-versus-undammed design with a forcing-conditioned estimand ---
comparing how dammed and hydroclimatically comparable undammed basins
respond to the \emph{same} drought forcing. This design targets the
long-term demand-side feedback that prior, hydrology-only analyses leave
open, and turns the socio-hydrological reservoir-effect hypothesis into
a falsifiable, causally framed test in the setting where it matters
most.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Study area and design}\label{study-area-and-design}

We study continental Chile (≈18--44° S), a strong north--south aridity
gradient spanning hyper-arid, Mediterranean, and humid-temperate
climates, over 2005--2024 --- the window in which reservoir storage,
meteorological forcing, and satellite outcomes jointly exist
(\texttt{config/study\_period.yml}). The design is a matched comparison
of \textbf{dammed versus undammed sub-watersheds}, with reservoir
presence treated as a basin-level intervention. Because 18 of 24
monitored reservoirs were commissioned before the storage record begins,
treatment is effectively time-invariant over the window and a
staggered-adoption (commissioning) event study is infeasible;
identification therefore rests on a matched cross-section combined with
conditioning on meteorological forcing rather than on calendar time.

The central estimand is the operational effect of a reservoir on drought
outcomes \emph{conditional on the covariates that govern where
reservoirs are sited} --- that is, whether dammed basins respond to the
same drought forcing differently from hydroclimatically comparable
undammed basins. This isolates reservoir \emph{operation} from reservoir
\emph{siting} \citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}.

\subsection{Spatial units and treatment
assignment}\label{spatial-units-and-treatment-assignment}

The analysis grain is the DGA BNA \textbf{sub-watershed (subcuenca)}
(467 units), chosen over the coarser cuenca grain (101 units) for power
and common support: reservoirs sit in Chile's largest main-stem basins,
and the finer grain supplies far more controls inside the treated size
range (\texttt{docs/design/grain-selection.md}). A subcuenca is
\textbf{treated} if it contains ≥1 of the 26 monitored reservoirs by
point-in-polygon assignment, giving \textbf{24 treated units}. An
untreated subcuenca that lies within a cuenca containing a reservoir is
flagged as \textbf{contaminated} --- it shares the regulated main stem
and is not a clean counterfactual --- and removed (45 units), leaving
\textbf{398 clean controls}. This contamination rule defuses the most
direct downstream-spillover (SUTVA) violation; residual adjacency and
shared-aquifer spillovers between neighbouring subcuencas are carried as
an identification caveat and checked at cuenca grain.

\subsection{Data}\label{data}

\textbf{Reservoir storage and treatment.} Monthly storage for 26
monitored reservoirs (2005-01 to 2026-04) with a per-reservoir capacity
column (\texttt{max\_level\_hm3}); storage and capacity share units
(hm³), so percent-of-capacity
(\texttt{100\ ×\ level\ /\ max\_level\_hm3}) is the
cross-reservoir-comparable state variable. Static attributes
(commissioning year, use, size class) come from the national dam
registry.

\textbf{Meteorological forcing.} Standardized drought indices --- SPI,
SPEI, and EDDI --- derived from CHIRPS precipitation and CHIRTS
temperature at timescales of 1/3/6/12/24/36 months, standardized on the
1991--2026 baseline (\texttt{config/variables.yml}). SPEI
\citep{Vicente-Serrano2010} at 12 months is the primary exogenous dose;
the suitability of CHIRPS-derived standardized indices for Chilean
drought monitoring is established, including ENSO-modulated drought
variability across the country
\citep{Zambrano2017, Meza2013, Oertel2020}. Conditioning on forcing ---
estimating effects on the deficit-to-impact \emph{slope} rather than on
levels --- is how we break the collinearity between the 2010-onset
megadrought and the treatment clock.

\textbf{Hydrological drought.} Daily streamflow from the CR2 network
(2000--2020) is aggregated to the Standardized Streamflow Index at 12
months {[}SSI-12; \citet{Vicente-Serrano2012c};
\citet{lema_technical_2025}{]}, the regulated-flow analogue of SPEI-12,
used as the outcome in the within-basin placebo (below). Gauges are
classified as upstream or downstream of each dam from an SRTM digital
elevation model.

\textbf{Ecological and land-use outcomes.} (i) annual irrigated/cropland
area from MapBiomas Chile Collection 2 (30 m, 1999--2024); (ii) actual
evapotranspiration from MODIS (8-day, \textasciitilde500 m, 2001--2024),
for whole basins and for an orchard stratum from the CIREN/ODEPA
cadastre; (iii) standardized vegetation and productivity anomalies ---
monthly z-score accumulated NDVI (zcNDVI-6), annual z-score NPP (zNPP),
and the standardized actual-ET anomaly (SETI) --- from MODIS over
2000--2024; the zcNDVI productivity proxy is validated for Chilean
agricultural drought, and the ecological response of Chilean vegetation
to the megadrought is well characterized
\citep{Zambrano2018, Glade2016, Venegas2022}.

\textbf{Matching covariates.} Baseline aridity (long-term mean annual
P/PET over the 1991--2020 WMO normal), watershed area, mean elevation
(SRTM), and Köppen--Geiger climate class, extracted per subcuenca by
zonal statistics.

\subsection{Matched control
construction}\label{matched-control-construction}

We estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) using
\textbf{entropy balancing} (\texttt{WeightIt},
\texttt{method\ =\ "ebal"}) exact-matched within Köppen main group,
balancing \textbf{log(area)} and \textbf{mean elevation}
(\texttt{docs/design/matched-controls.md}). Balancing on size and
elevation rather than latitude is deliberate: latitude and the climate
class fight each other across the Andes--Patagonia span, and
substituting SRTM elevation raised the effective control sample size
from ≈9 to ≈91 while balancing latitude for free.

The procedure retains \textbf{21 of 24 treated subcuencas} (three
high-Andes units fall outside local elevation common support) and
achieves essentially perfect balance --- standardized mean differences
for log(area) and elevation fall from 1.18 / 0.37 to ≈0.000, variance
ratios ≈1 --- with an \textbf{effective control sample size ≈91} out of
244 in-window controls. Baseline aridity is a genuine confound (dammed
basins are markedly more arid: median P/PET 0.26 vs 0.80) but is
\emph{not} used as a balance target, because hard-balancing it collapses
the ESS from 91 to 19; instead the residual aridity imbalance (SMD
≈0.17, above the 0.1 target) is absorbed by doubly-robust regression
adjustment. Because the matched set does not fully balance aridity, this
adjustment is \emph{load-bearing} rather than cosmetic, and the
estimates can be sensitive to its functional form; we therefore
stress-test the aridity-adjusted estimates with a quadratic aridity term
and report the curvature-robust values where feasible (see Results).
Robustness to the matching method is assessed with coarsened exact
matching (CEM) and 1:2 nearest-neighbour (Mahalanobis) matching on the
identical common-support sample; entropy balancing and NN agree on all
21 treated, while CEM additionally prunes the 4 most extreme large/high
basins, so all treatment effects are reported with and without those
units.

\subsection{Causal estimands and
estimators}\label{causal-estimands-and-estimators}

\textbf{Doubly-robust matched ATT.} Each outcome is estimated three ways
on the same sample --- weighting-only (ebal-weighted difference in
means), regression-only (covariate-adjusted g-computation), and the
headline \textbf{doubly-robust} estimator (ebal weights \emph{and} an
outcome model in log(area), elevation, and log(aridity)) --- so
functional-form sensitivity is visible. Continuous covariates are
centred at the treated mean so the treatment coefficient reads as the
ATT; standard errors are HC3-robust (ebal weights treated as fixed, the
standard mildly anti-conservative simplification).

\textbf{Forcing-conditioned transmission slope.} To remove megadrought
\emph{exposure} (concentrated in exactly the arid basins where
reservoirs sit), the outcome for the DR machinery is the per-basin
\textbf{transmission slope} --- the regression of an annual outcome
(e.g.~zNPP) on annual SPEI-12 over 2005--2024 --- a standardized,
cross-basin drought-transmission elasticity. The vulnerability
hypothesis predicts a \emph{steeper} slope (more impact per unit
deficit) in dammed basins.

\textbf{Forcing-interacted difference-in-differences.} On the observed
area and ET panels we fit

\[
y_{it} = \beta\,(\text{dammed}_i \times \text{SPEI}_{it}) + \gamma\,\text{SPEI}_{it}
         + \alpha_i + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{it},
\]

with subcuenca (\(\alpha_i\)) and year (\(\tau_t\)) fixed effects and
entropy-balancing weights. Because treatment is time-invariant, the dose
is meteorological SPEI rather than calendar year: the year fixed effects
absorb the common megadrought shock, and \(\beta\) is the differential
deficit-to-impact slope between dammed and matched-control basins ---
robust to the siting-in-arid-central-Chile confound, which biases levels
far more than slopes. The vulnerability hypothesis predicts
\(\beta > 0\).

\textbf{Equivalence testing and minimum detectable effect.} Because the
headline results are nulls, we move beyond failure-to-reject and bound
the operational effect. For each null we report a two one-sided test
(TOST): the effect is declared equivalent to zero if its 90\% confidence
interval lies within a negligible region of ±25\% of the baseline
(untreated) transmission slope. The minimum detectable effect (at 80\%
power, two-sided \(\alpha=0.05\)) and the equivalence interval are built
from the \textbf{standard deviation of the permutation-null
distribution}, not the analytic standard error, since cluster-robust SEs
over-reject at \textasciitilde21 clusters
(\texttt{src/R/causal/equivalence.R}). To confirm the null is
informative rather than underpowered, we additionally run a
\textbf{positive control}: a known buffering slope is injected into the
treated units and the full estimator and randomization inference are
re-run, verifying that the design recovers an effect of policy-relevant
size and correctly flags none when none is injected.

\textbf{Siting-confound decomposition.} To quantify how much of any
apparent buffering is selection, we fit a ladder on the same panel: (1)
a naive pooled slope gap (no weights, no fixed effects --- what a
conventional dammed-vs-undammed study reports); (2) unit + time fixed
effects, unweighted; (3) the design estimator (ebal weights + fixed
effects). The shrinkage from (1) to (3) is the siting confound; the
residual at (3) is the regulation estimate.

\textbf{Within-basin upstream/downstream placebo.} The primary
selection-versus-effect discriminator contrasts the SPEI-12→SSI-12
transmission slope at regulated \textbf{downstream} reaches against
physically unregulated \textbf{upstream} reaches of the \emph{same}
dammed basins, which share climate, geology, and selection history. A
genuine regulation effect implies a downstream-specific dose-response
that the upstream placebo does not reproduce; an attenuation equally
present upstream is siting, not regulation. Of the 21 treated matched
basins, 15 have both an upstream and a downstream gauge, giving
near-national coverage rather than a single case study. The contrast is
also split by Köppen region to test whether a regulation effect emerges
in the high-supply south. Because upstream gauges sit at higher
elevations than downstream gauges, we test the placebo's comparability
assumption directly: we quantify the elevation gap and regress the
per-gauge transmission slope on elevation among undammed control gauges,
so that any elevation-driven regime difference is measured rather than
assumed.

\subsection{Inference}\label{inference}

With only \textasciitilde21 treated clusters, cluster-robust standard
errors over-reject, so the primary inference is \textbf{randomization
inference}: the treatment label is permuted among units within Köppen ×
aridity- tercile strata (≥999 permutations) and the observed statistic
is compared against the resulting null distribution; cluster-robust
confidence intervals are reported alongside for completeness. Parallel
pre-trends are checked with a dynamic event study (dammed × year,
reference 2009), and residual confounding is probed with an aridity
placebo that relabels the most-arid control tercile as pseudo-treated.

\subsection{Software and
reproducibility}\label{software-and-reproducibility}

All analyses are implemented in R within a \texttt{targets} pipeline
(matched-set construction, estimators, and figures are individual
targets), using \texttt{WeightIt}/\texttt{cobalt} for balancing and
diagnostics, \texttt{fixest} for fixed-effects regression,
\texttt{sf}/\texttt{terra}/\texttt{exactextractr} for the spatial
extractions, and \texttt{renv} for package pinning. Every result in the
manuscript corresponds to a named pipeline target; no figure or table is
produced by hand.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Reservoirs mark, but do not make, irrigated
vulnerability}\label{reservoirs-mark-but-do-not-make-irrigated-vulnerability}

Across the matched design (21 dammed vs 244 control subcuencas,
effective control sample ≈91 after entropy balancing), dammed basins
contain roughly an order of magnitude more cropland than matched
controls --- a mean of 11.1\% of basin area versus 2.4\%
(Figure~\ref{fig-area}, panel a). This gap, however, is a \emph{siting}
signature: it is present and stable across the entire 2000--2024 record,
with dammed and control trajectories evolving in parallel through the
2010-onset megadrought (dashed line). A dynamic event study confirms
this --- the dammed × year coefficients on cropland area are jointly
flat before 2009 (Wald \(p = 0.68\)) and show no divergence after the
drought onset (Figure~\ref{fig-area}, panel b). Reservoirs mark where
irrigated agriculture already was; they do not detectably expand it or
change how it responds to drought.

\subsection{No operational reservoir effect survives once siting is
matched
out}\label{no-operational-reservoir-effect-survives-once-siting-is-matched-out}

We probe the operational vulnerability mechanism with four independent
estimators (Table~\ref{tbl-main}): two cross-sectional doubly-robust
matched ATTs --- net irrigated-area expansion (\(-0.69\) ha
km\textsuperscript{-2}, 95\% CI \([-2.4, 1.1]\)) and reservoir
evapotranspiration buffering (\(-0.014\) d log ET / d SPEI,
\([-0.065, 0.037]\)) --- and forcing-interacted
difference-in-differences slope gaps on the observed area and ET panels,
in which the deficit dose is the meteorological SPEI rather than
calendar time (so year fixed effects absorb the common megadrought shock
and the estimand is the differential deficit-to-impact \emph{slope}).
Because the treatment is effectively time-invariant (18 of 24 reservoirs
predate the record), this within-unit, forcing-based design is the only
defensible dynamic identification; inference uses randomization of
treatment within climate × aridity strata (\textasciitilde21 treated
clusters).

Under the design's primary inference, no estimate indicates an
operational effect (Figure~\ref{fig-forest}, Table~\ref{tbl-main}).
Three of the four straddle zero outright: net irrigated-area expansion,
and the slope gaps for irrigated area (\(p_{\text{perm}} = 0.91\)) and
orchard ET (\(p_{\text{perm}} = 0.24\)). The whole-basin ET slope gap is
the exception, and we treat it explicitly rather than fold it silently
into the nulls. Its cluster-robust interval is positive and excludes
zero (\(+0.048\), \([0.026, 0.070]\)); however, it fails the design's
pre-specified randomization inference (\(p_{\text{perm}} = 0.11\)) and,
decisively, its dynamic event study shows strongly non-parallel
pre-trends (\(p \approx 10^{-10}\)). The panel is therefore confounded
--- whole-basin MODIS ET is contaminated by residual aridity over
sparsely vegetated, barren land --- so neither its point estimate nor
its cluster-robust interval is interpretable, and for the same reason it
is the one outcome we exclude from the equivalence bounding below. Two
facts keep this exception from rescuing a buffering narrative: taken at
face value the estimate points toward \emph{vulnerability} (a steeper
deficit-to-impact slope), not buffering; and the cleaner orchard-ET
stratum and the cross-sectional ET-buffering ATT both straddle zero.
Across the interpretable outcomes, then, no panel shows the positive
slope the vulnerability mechanism predicts nor the negative slope
operational buffering would produce: conditional on siting, the
operational reservoir effect brackets zero.

\subsection{Sensitivity to aridity
adjustment}\label{sensitivity-to-aridity-adjustment}

Dammed basins are systematically more arid than controls, and the
matched set does not fully remove this: baseline aridity remains
imbalanced after weighting (standardized mean difference ≈\(0.17\),
above the \(0.1\) target), so the doubly-robust adjustment for aridity
is load-bearing rather than a cosmetic mop-up. Because dammed basins are
the more arid, residual under-control biases \emph{toward} apparent
vulnerability, which makes the nulls conservative. Functional form
matters and we report it: in the ecological-proxy transmission-slope
analysis the dammed-versus- control estimate falls from \(+0.33\) under
linear aridity adjustment to \(+0.11\) once a quadratic aridity term is
added, and in the irrigation-specific stratum it collapses to ≈\(0\)
(\(0.08 \rightarrow 0.01\)). The apparent steepening is therefore
largely a water-limitation/aridity artifact --- consistent with the
siting interpretation --- but the same sensitivity means estimates that
rely on linear aridity control should be read as
functional-form-dependent, and we treat the curvature-robust values as
the conservative reference.

\subsection{Bounding the null: what the design can rule
out}\label{bounding-the-null-what-the-design-can-rule-out}

A null is only informative against a stated effect size, so we convert
each non-result into a bound via equivalence testing (two one-sided
tests) and a minimum detectable effect (MDE) built from the
permutation-null spread (Table~\ref{tbl-equiv}). We bound the four
interpretable outcomes (streamflow ITT and downstream, irrigated-area
DiD, orchard-ET DiD); whole-basin ET is excluded because its pre-trends
fail, so an equivalence interval on a confounded estimate would be
meaningless. The streamflow design has the most power: it can detect a
buffering effect that attenuates SPEI→SSI transmission by ≳46\% of the
baseline slope (MDE \(0.27\) on a baseline of \(0.59\); downstream-only
\(0.31\), ≳59\%). No outcome's 90\% interval falls inside its
pre-specified negligible region (±25\% of baseline), so we do \emph{not}
claim formal statistical equivalence to zero --- with \textasciitilde21
treated clusters the design can exclude only \textbf{large} operational
effects, not small ones --- a policy-relevant buffer of 20--45\% of
baseline transmission is \emph{not} excluded. The causal weight of the
null therefore rests on the within-basin placebo below, not on
equivalence: the design excludes only large operational effects, and the
placebo indicates that the residual apparent attenuation is selection
rather than regulation.

\subsection{The apparent buffering is reservoir siting, not
regulation}\label{the-apparent-buffering-is-reservoir-siting-not-regulation}

The preceding nulls use vegetation and ET proxies. We obtain the same
answer on the direct, vegetation-independent availability outcome ---
streamflow --- and, uniquely here, can show \emph{why} a naive analysis
would mislead. From CR2 daily gauge records (2000--2020) we build a
12-month Standardized Streamflow Index (SSI-12) and test whether
meteorological drought (SPEI-12) propagates less strongly to streamflow
drought in dammed than in matched-control basins
(Figure~\ref{fig-streamflow}). Dammed basins do show a flatter SPEI→SSI
transmission --- the visual signature of buffering
(Figure~\ref{fig-streamflow}, panel a). But this apparent attenuation
\textbf{(i)} does not survive randomization inference (intent-to-treat
slope gap \(-0.18\), cluster \(p = 0.03\) but
\(p_{\text{perm}} = 0.39\)), and \textbf{(ii)} is present just as
strongly at gauges located \emph{upstream} of the dam --- which are
physically unregulated --- as at downstream gauges (\(-0.20\) vs
\(-0.16\); Figure~\ref{fig-streamflow}, panel b). There is no
downstream-versus-upstream dose-response, the signature a genuine
regulation effect would require. The flatter slope is therefore a
property of \emph{where} reservoirs are sited --- arid,
hydrogeologically distinct basins --- not of the reservoirs themselves.

The siting interpretation is confirmed by the way the signal behaves
across the aridity gradient. The national ``buffering'' slope gap
(\(-0.16\) to \(-0.20\)) \textbf{collapses to ≈ \(-0.03\) once the
comparison is made within climate region} --- the textbook signature of
a between-region confound, in which arid treated basins are contrasted
against a control pool spanning the whole wet→dry gradient. In the
high-supply south, where buffering should be easiest to detect, the
downstream and upstream gaps are statistically identical (\(-0.036\) vs
\(-0.034\)) and both indistinguishable from zero (southern downstream
\(p_{\text{perm}} = 0.78\)). Splitting by region thus both kills the
signal and equalizes the upstream and downstream reaches --- two
independent confirmations that the attenuation is the aridity gradient,
not regulation. Because the upstream-of-dam comparison is unavailable
for any vegetation outcome, streamflow provides the cleanest
within-design falsification: the effect a naive dammed-versus-control or
calendar-time analysis would report is siting, not regulation.

The placebo assumes upstream and downstream reaches are hydrologically
comparable, which we test directly (Table~\ref{tbl-placebo}). Upstream
gauges do sit higher than downstream ones (mean \(1.0\) vs \(0.46\) km),
and gauge elevation is not innocuous --- among undammed control gauges
the SPEI→SSI transmission slope declines by \(0.21\) per kilometre of
elevation (\(p < 0.001\)). The placebo nonetheless holds, for two
reasons. First, the \emph{raw} transmission slopes of upstream and
downstream treated gauges are nearly identical (\(0.565\) vs \(0.599\)),
so the elevation difference does not produce a transmission-regime gap
of the size at issue. Second, the apparent buffering is if anything
\emph{stronger} upstream (\(-0.20\)) than downstream (\(-0.16\)) --- the
opposite of what regulation, which acts only downstream, would produce.
We cannot claim the reaches are perfectly interchangeable, and a
residual elevation/regime component remains a caveat, but neither the
raw slopes nor the direction of the gap supports a regulation effect
masked by elevation.

\subsection{Storage declines without a loss of buffering
capacity}\label{storage-declines-without-a-loss-of-buffering-capacity}

If reservoirs neither buffer nor amplify drought operationally, the
relevant change under the megadrought is in how much water is available
to store (Figure~\ref{fig-storage}). Over 2005--2024 the entire storage
band drifts downward in step: annual peak storage declines by \(-1.3\)
percentage points of capacity per year (\(p = 0.013\)) and the annual
trough falls almost identically (\(-1.2\) pp yr\textsuperscript{-1},
\(p < 10^{-4}\)), while the seasonal amplitude is statistically
unchanged (\(-0.06\) pp yr\textsuperscript{-1}, \(p = 0.91\)). A band
that drops without narrowing is consistent with a supply-side level
shift rather than a degraded buffer, which would compress the amplitude;
reservoirs still convert meteorological surplus into storage as
efficiently as before. We caution that this storage trend is descriptive
--- a pooled within-reservoir trend with no control group or
naturalized-inflow series --- so it cannot by itself distinguish
declining inflow from rising demand-side drawdown. Read alongside
Chile's documented aridification, however, the pattern is more
consistent with a falling water supply than with any loss of storage or
buffering capacity, and on that reading the operational case for
expanding storage as drought protection is weak
\citep{li_diminishing_2023, wang_reservoir_2024}.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Across a matched national design and four independent estimators ---
cross-sectional irrigated-area expansion and ET buffering, and
forcing-interacted slope gaps on observed area and ET --- the
operational effect of reservoirs on drought outcomes brackets zero once
siting is matched out. The same answer holds for the direct,
vegetation-independent availability outcome: the apparent flattening of
meteorological-to-streamflow drought propagation in dammed basins is as
strong at physically unregulated \emph{upstream} reaches as downstream,
and collapses from a slope gap of \(-0.18\) to ≈\(-0.03\) when the
comparison is made within climate region. Dammed basins do hold roughly
an order of magnitude more cropland than matched controls, but this gap
is fixed across the record, with parallel pre-trends and no divergence
through the megadrought. Taken together, the evidence supports a single
interpretation: reservoirs \textbf{mark} where water-dependent
vulnerability already concentrated --- arid, already-irrigated basins
--- rather than \textbf{make} it. The vulnerability signature is a
property of siting, set before the drought and stable through it, not a
dynamic consequence of regulation.

This reframes ``no effect'' as the finding rather than a failure of
power, but only because a credible \emph{cause} of the apparent effect
is identified. The within-basin upstream/downstream placebo is the
cleanest within-design falsification here: a genuine regulation effect
must appear downstream of the dam and be absent upstream, and it is not.
What remains, once that placebo and within-region conditioning are
applied, is the aridity gradient itself. The methodological corollary is
general. A naive dammed--control contrast, or a calendar-time
difference-in-differences, would read the large cross-sectional area,
ET, and transmission gaps --- amplified during the drought --- as a
reservoir effect; conditioning on realized meteorological forcing and
estimating a deficit-to-impact \emph{slope}, and benchmarking against an
unregulated within-basin reach, makes the signal disappear.
Reservoir-buffering studies that compare dammed against undammed basins,
or pre- against post-construction levels, inherit the full siting
confound
\citep{lopezmoreno_dam_2009, wu_non-linear_2017, wu_impacts_2018, schilstra_effects_2024, lee_drought_2026};
the upstream placebo is a transferable identification tool applicable to
any regulated river with gauges above and below the structure.

If reservoirs neither buffer nor amplify drought operationally, the
relevant change under a multi-decadal dry spell is in the water
available to store rather than in the structures, and the storage record
is suggestive on this point. The seasonal refill remains efficient and
its amplitude is unchanged, yet the entire storage band drifts downward,
peak and trough declining in step at roughly \(-1.3\) and \(-1.2\)
percentage points of capacity per year. A band that drops without
narrowing is consistent with a falling supply ceiling rather than a
degraded buffer. We stress that this is a descriptive, control-free
trend that cannot on its own separate declining inflow from rising
demand-side drawdown \citep{condon_evapotranspiration_2020}; both would
shift the band downward without narrowing it. Read against Chile's
documented transition from episodic drought toward structural
aridification --- in which declining precipitation and rising
atmospheric evaporative demand lower inflows independently of any
infrastructure
\citep{zambrano_drought_2025, Vicente-Serrano2022, vicenteserrano_unraveling_2019, garreaud_20102015_2017, AlvarezGarreton2021},
echoing aridification trajectories documented elsewhere
\citep{overpeck_climate_2020} --- and the global pattern in which
reservoirs increasingly fail to refill and the storage returns of new
dam construction are diminishing
\citep{wang_reservoir_2024, li_diminishing_2023}, the more likely
reading is that the binding constraint is shifting toward water supply
rather than storage capacity or operation. A naturalized-inflow series
and a demand-side decomposition are needed to settle this, and we treat
it as a strong hypothesis rather than an identified result.

The policy implication is direct and uncomfortable for the prevailing
response to scarcity in Chile and elsewhere. Because the 1981 Water Code
makes existing water-use rights perpetual private property that the
state cannot readily curtail during drought, the reflexive response has
been supply-side --- a national programme of new dams and inter-basin
transfers \citep{Barria2021, Garreaud2025, macpherson_evolving_2023}.
Our results imply that adding storage to basins whose inflow is
declining is a supply-blind response to a supply problem: it cannot
manufacture water that aridification is removing, and the buffering
benefit it is built to provide is, on this evidence, attributable to
where dams sit rather than to what they do. This is the drought-domain
expression of the reservoir-effect and supply--demand-cycle hypotheses
\citep{di_baldassarre_water_2018}, whose practical force has been
contested for want of quantification \citep{kellner_controversial_2021};
we provide a credibly identified test and find the protective function
over-attributed. The leverage instead lies on the demand and allocation
side --- curbing the over-allocation and induced expansion that the
efficiency paradox entrenches
\citep{grafton_paradox_2018, reade_malagueno_libre_2024, jordan_adaptation_2025}
--- and in managed recharge and reallocation rather than new
impoundments
\citep{rivera-vidal_managed_2025, rivera_legal_2016, scanlon_global_2023}.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, treatment is
effectively time-invariant over the record (most reservoirs predate it),
so we identify differential drought \emph{response} rather than the
effect of switching a reservoir on; a staggered-commissioning design is
infeasible here and would be the cleaner counterfactual where
construction dates and longer records permit. Second, the result is a
bounded null, not a proven equivalence: with \textasciitilde21 treated
clusters the design rules out buffering larger than ≈46--59\% of
baseline transmission for streamflow but cannot exclude small
operational effects, so the causal weight rests on the placebo, not on
equivalence. Third, the observed-area outcome (MapBiomas class 18)
captures total cropland rather than irrigation status, and Chile's
agricultural change has been compositional --- a shift toward perennial
orchards within roughly constant cropland area --- so a latent
water-demand signal not visible in net area remains possible;
whole-basin MODIS ET is additionally confounded by residual aridity over
barren land, so the ET conclusion leans on the cross-sectional buffering
null and the orchard stratum --- both low-powered (minimum detectable
effects of roughly twice their baselines), so for ET specifically the
finding is ``no \emph{large} effect detected'' rather than no effect.
Relatedly, the matched set leaves baseline aridity imbalanced (SMD
≈0.17), so the doubly-robust adjustment for it is load-bearing and the
estimates are sensitive to its functional form: a quadratic aridity term
moves the proxy transmission-slope estimate from \(+0.33\) to \(+0.11\)
--- toward the null we report, and a reminder that the linear-adjustment
values are not the conservative ones. Fourth, the streamflow placebo
covers 15 of 21 treated basins over 2000--2020, and the high-supply
southern subset is small; a modest regulation effect in wetter basins
cannot be excluded, though drought is also least binding there. Upstream
and downstream gauges are also not perfectly interchangeable ---
upstream gauges sit higher, and elevation predicts the transmission
slope among controls --- so a residual regime component remains; that
said, the raw upstream and downstream transmission slopes are nearly
identical and the apparent buffering is if anything stronger upstream,
neither of which is consistent with a regulation effect obscured by
elevation. Finally, residual SUTVA spillovers (adjacency, shared
aquifers) survive the contamination rule, and our estimand is the
\emph{drought} effect of reservoirs --- not their value for hydropower,
flood control, or urban supply, which this study does not assess.

One alternative interpretation deserves emphasis because mean-based
estimators cannot see it: damming might leave the \emph{magnitude} of
drought impact unchanged while recomposing its \textbf{dependence
structure}, weakening the meteorology→outcome coupling and substituting
a storage→outcome coupling --- a regime shift that would itself explain
the convergent null. We cannot adjudicate this with the present data:
the storage-recovery dynamics are stationary (refill efficiency and
amplitude are flat), which weakens the premise, and the decisive test
requires reservoir release and operating-rule records that are not yet
available; only a drawdown proxy is on disk. Absent that information,
and given that any apparent decoupling must survive the upstream
placebo, the dependence-shift account reduces to the siting
interpretation. Resolving it --- together with longer streamflow records
and water-rights allocation data --- is the priority for future work.

Returning to the question that motivates the study --- do reservoirs
create resilience or merely postpone vulnerability under prolonged
drought? --- our evidence indicates that, under aridification, they do
neither operationally: storage neither buffers nor amplifies the drought
signal beyond what basin siting already implies, while the water
available to store steadily declines. Resilience to a falling supply
ceiling will not come from more impoundments but from confronting demand
and allocation --- the harder, and unavoidable, adaptation.
